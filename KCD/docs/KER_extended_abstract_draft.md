## Title
Early Diagnosis of Small-Business Life Cycles from Weekly Transaction Data: Evidence from Seoul's Food-Service Sector

## Extended Abstract
This paper asks whether small businesses actually follow the conventional start-up-growth-maturity-decline life cycle that underlies many policy programs, and whether high-frequency transaction data can identify economically meaningful trajectories early enough to improve targeting. In Korea, support programs for small businesses are commonly organized around stylized life-cycle stages, but the empirical basis for those stages is usually drawn from surveys and self-reported conditions rather than observed business behavior. We address this gap by developing a data-driven framework for measuring heterogeneous post-entry trajectories using weekly sales records.

The analysis uses weekly transaction data from Korean Credit Data for Seoul establishments. Importantly, the underlying metadata indicate that the full store universe in this project belongs to the food-service sector, including Korean, Japanese, Chinese, Western, fast-food, cafe, bakery/dessert, and pub businesses. The full metadata cover 59,089 establishments, of which 50,635 have enough observed weeks for full-sample classification. For entry-cohort analysis, we focus on establishments opened after January 2019 and align each business by weeks since opening rather than calendar time. To absorb common shocks such as COVID-19 restrictions and other aggregate fluctuations, establishment-level sales are normalized by aggregate sales in the same relative week and then scaled within store. The main entry-cohort samples contain 14,347 establishments observed for 108 weeks and 8,562 establishments observed for 162 weeks.

The empirical strategy has three parts. First, we recover common sales-path patterns from weekly time series using clustering and shape-based trajectory methods designed for noisy economic data. Second, we convert these paths into interpretable life-cycle states using the direction of sales changes before and after major turning points, together with current status. In the entry-cohort setting this yields codes such as DDZ and UUX; in the data-driven specification it also yields compact six-class states such as persistent decline, rebound, sustained growth, and post-growth deterioration. Third, we evaluate whether trajectory information improves prediction beyond raw levels by estimating supervised models with store characteristics, customer-composition variables, delivery share, local competitive context, and early sales-path features.

The results indicate that the canonical hump-shaped life cycle is a poor description of small-business dynamics. In the 108-week entrant sample, the dominant pattern is a persistent decline followed by an exit-stage profile (DDZ), which accounts for 8,956 of 14,347 establishments, or about 62 percent. By contrast, rise-then-stabilize trajectories are relatively rare. At the same time, a broader full-sample observed-window analysis shows that business dynamics are not uniformly pessimistic: among 50,635 establishments with sufficient histories, 40.0 percent are classified as Growth, 38.2 percent as Stable, and 21.7 percent as Decline. This contrast suggests that entrant dynamics and incumbent dynamics should be analyzed separately rather than collapsed into a single life-cycle template.

The findings also show that economically interpretable trajectory features materially improve prediction. In a six-class lifecycle prediction exercise using the data-driven labels, a baseline model using only conventional store-level variables achieves a weighted F1-score of 0.43, whereas adding trajectory-shape information raises performance to 0.73; adding customer-behavior variables keeps performance at roughly the same level (weighted F1 about 0.73). In complementary early forecasting exercises, using only the first 30 weeks of data already produces substantial predictive content: the best three-class model reaches weighted F1 of 0.572 and accuracy of 0.573, compared with a majority-class baseline weighted F1 of 0.287 and accuracy of 0.457. Across specifications, trend slope is the strongest and most stable predictor of later outcomes, while new-customer share is positively associated with Growth and delivery intensity is more closely associated with Decline. Supplementary full-sample age-bucket analyses further show that the explanatory role of customer acquisition becomes stronger after the earliest business stage.

This paper contributes to the economics of small-business dynamics in three ways. First, it offers an objective, high-frequency alternative to survey-based measurement of business life cycles. Second, it documents substantial heterogeneity in post-entry and incumbent trajectories within Seoul's food-service sector, challenging the assumption that firms move along a single linear developmental path. Third, it shows that interpretable trajectory states and early-path features have predictive value for later business outcomes. The policy implication is that small-business support should be based less on broad stylized stages and more on observed trajectory types and early-warning indicators. A transaction-data framework of this kind can help policymakers distinguish businesses that are persistently deteriorating from those that are likely to stabilize or recover, thereby improving the timing and targeting of intervention.
