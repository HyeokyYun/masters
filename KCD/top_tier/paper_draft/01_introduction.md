## Draft v1

Small business closure is not only a private loss for the entrepreneur but a systemic cost for local economies, commercial districts, and employment ecosystems. In Korea alone, approximately 800,000 self-employed businesses closed in 2023 — a 13% year-over-year increase amid post-pandemic recovery — with food service establishments accounting for nearly a quarter of exits (KOSIS, 2024). The downstream consequences compound through supplier networks, landlord insolvencies, and neighborhood revitalization decay, placing small business resilience squarely within the scope of information systems for social good (Majchrzak, Markus, & Wareham, 2016; Pan, Pan, & Leidner, 2012). Yet, despite decades of research on entrepreneurial survival, the empirical foundations of small business lifecycle prediction remain surprisingly thin. Most existing studies rely on either (a) cross-sectional surveys of owners that capture *ex post* rationalizations rather than behavioral signals (Cooper, Gimeno-Gascon, & Woo, 1994; Shepherd, 2003), or (b) annual financial statements that are unavailable for the vast majority of micro-enterprises. A third methodological concern — survivorship bias — is especially acute: lifecycle panels systematically exclude stores that close early, inflating the apparent success rate and distorting inferences about what distinguishes growers from decliners (Denrell, 2003).

This study addresses these gaps by leveraging a large-scale, high-frequency panel of card-transaction data. We analyze weekly revenue and customer flow for **59,089 urban restaurant establishments** in Seoul over 2021-01-01 to 2023-08-28 (6.58 million store-weeks), provided by Korea Credit Data (KCD). The granularity of this dataset — behaviorally observed rather than self-reported — makes it possible, for the first time, to model small business lifecycles as continuous trajectories rather than discrete states, and to test causal hypotheses about leading indicators that previous cross-sectional designs could not adjudicate. Specifically, we ask three linked research questions. **(RQ1)** What dynamic signals, observable within the first 30 weeks of a store's panel window, reliably distinguish stores that will subsequently enter a Growth, Stable, or Decline trajectory over the next 30 weeks? **(RQ2)** Does a hybrid representation that combines hand-engineered time-series features with temporal-clustering-derived state variables and change-point features outperform end-to-end deep sequence models in this setting? **(RQ3)** Is there causal evidence that new-customer inflow precedes, rather than merely correlates with, sales rebounds — and if so, can this "Golden Cross" signal be deployed as a decision support artifact for policy intervention?

Our approach follows the Design Science Research paradigm (Hevner, March, Park, & Ram, 2004; Peffers, Tuunanen, Rothenberger, & Chatterjee, 2007): we build and rigorously evaluate an **Early Warning System (EWS) artifact** that produces calibrated, store-level 30-week-ahead risk scores, while simultaneously contributing to the theoretical understanding of small business lifecycles. Methodologically, we combine survival analysis (Kaplan-Meier curves, Cox proportional hazards), temporal clustering (K-Means on aligned sequences, K-Shape for shape-invariant groupings), causal identification triangulation (store-level Granger causality tests, propensity-score matched difference-in-differences with placebo robustness checks, two-way fixed-effects panel regression), and supervised prediction with interpretability analysis (XGBoost with SHAP decomposition, benchmarked against LSTM, GRU, and Transformer sequence models under identical cross-validation protocols). To ensure that our findings are not artifacts of questionable modeling choices, we execute an extensive self-audit: cross-fold cluster leakage tests, Cox proportional-hazards assumption checks via Schoenfeld residuals, closure-cutoff and outcome-threshold sensitivity analyses, and an enhanced PSM specification that reduces pre-treatment level gaps by 78% while preserving DiD effect stability.

Four contributions emerge. **First**, we quantify the magnitude of survivorship bias in lifecycle panels — the closure rate outside the 52-week panel window is 52%, versus 10% within it, a 5-fold gap that challenges conclusions from prior literature reliant on panel-surviving stores. **Second**, we resolve an empirical paradox between Cox-PH-estimated volatility hazard (HR=1.11) and observed volatility-growth coexistence by decomposing it into four mechanisms: phase-dependent volatility (Growth exceeds Decline only in the early window, reversing thereafter), an inverted-U shape across volatility deciles (peak Growth rate at the 5th decile), outcome-specific hazard heterogeneity (volatility is protective within Growth but hazardous within Decline), and residual survivorship confounding. **Third**, we demonstrate that a *hybrid* representation — hand-engineered features augmented by K-Means/K-Shape cluster assignments and change-point features — achieves Macro-F1 = 0.648 and AUC = 0.830, a 25% F1 improvement over the best multivariate deep sequence baseline (LSTM/GRU/Transformer on 5-channel input). This reverses the common assumption that end-to-end sequence learning dominates hand-crafted representations at this data scale. **Fourth**, we deliver the EWS artifact itself: calibrated risk scores for 49,007 stores with operating-point and cost-sensitive threshold analyses, providing actionable decision support for KCD, municipal governments, and small business associations. The artifact's policy utility is evaluated via simulated benefit/cost scenarios, identifying an optimal intervention threshold and quantifying net utility at 45,412 units.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews prior work on small business survival, early warning systems in finance, and time-series representation learning. Section 3 describes the KCD dataset and our outcome labeling scheme. Section 4 presents the methodology. Section 5 reports empirical results, organized by research question. Section 6 discusses theoretical and practical implications, acknowledges limitations (parallel trends partial violation, domain specificity, Cox PH assumption violations for three covariates), and outlines cross-domain extension and deployment directions. Section 7 concludes.
